Here's a question I ask every student who comes through Hair B&B University: how many foil techniques do you actually use on a regular basis?

Most stylists answer honestly. Two, maybe three. A slice when they want something clean. A weave for fullness. A zigzag when they want a softer root. And they've been cycling through the same rotation since cosmetology school, because nobody ever sat them down and showed them the full picture.

There are eight distinct foil highlight stitch methods that belong in every professional colorist's vocabulary. Understanding all eight doesn't mean you'll use all eight every week. It means you'll know which one to reach for when a specific client sits down with a specific goal.

Why Your Foil Technique Matters More Than Your Product

I hear stylists debate bleach brands constantly. Developer ratios, processing times, integrity scores — and all of that matters. But the truth is, two colorists can use the exact same lightener on the exact same starting level and produce completely different results based solely on how they take their sections and place their foils.

The product gets you to the level. The technique determines what that level looks like on the hair.

A beautifully lifted section placed with a slice will look completely different from the same lifted section placed with a babylight weave — different texture, different root, different movement, different coverage. Same chemistry. The foil placement made the difference. This is why stylists who invest in expanding their foiling vocabulary consistently outperform those who stay in a two-method comfort zone — in results, in the range of clients they can serve, and in the price points they can command.

Stitch 1: The Slice

Stitch 01

The Slice

What it is: A slice takes every strand of hair within the section into the foil — no weaving. The entire thickness of hair between two partings is isolated and lightened.

What it produces: Maximum coverage and high contrast. Slices are bright, bold, and graphic. They create clean, chunky pieces of color with visible separation from the natural base.

What to watch: Because slices lift every strand, they can look dense and heavy if overused. Best used in targeted placement — face framing, crown panels — rather than as the only technique throughout a full head.

Best ForClients who want high-impact brightness, dramatic contrast, or an editorial look. Also useful in correction when you need to fully lift a specific section.

Stitch 2: The Chunky Weave

Stitch 02

The Chunky Weave

What it is: A weave where the comb picks up larger, thicker sections to go into the foil, leaving thicker sections out. The weave alternates "in" and "out" at a coarser scale than a traditional weave.

What it produces: Bold, visible highlights with clear separation between lighter and darker sections. Less graphic than a slice because some hair is left out, but still high-contrast.

What to watch: Can look dated if not balanced with strong tone or intentional face-framing. Work carefully on fine or thin hair — large patches of lifted color can create the appearance of reduced density.

Best ForClients who want dimension with visible, chunky brightness. Great for very thick hair where significant lift needs to be distributed throughout the section.

Stitch 3: The Traditional Weave

Stitch 03

The Traditional Weave

What it is: The standard foil weave most stylists learn first. The comb alternates picking up fine-to-medium sections — into the foil, left out, into the foil — creating an even, regular pattern throughout the section.

What it produces: The classic highlight look. Even distribution, consistent coverage, predictable result. What most clients picture when they ask for a "full head of highlights."

What to watch: The regular pattern can read as "done" rather than "grown-in" — especially at the root line. For clients who want a more natural result, this technique benefits from pairing with a softer root approach.

Best ForClients who want consistent, uniform coverage. The most adaptable technique to different foil densities — more foils equals more coverage.

Stitch 4: Babylights

Stitch 04

Babylights

What it is: An ultra-fine weave that takes very small, delicate sections — typically one to three strands — into the foil, leaving the majority of hair out.

What it produces: The finest, most natural highlight possible. Babylights create the effect of natural childhood hair — sunlight that kissed individual strands. Incredibly dimensional, especially on finer hair.

What to watch: Babylights are time-intensive and should be priced accordingly. On very dark or coarse hair, the subtlety may not deliver the lift clients expect. For choosing the right lightener for a babylight service, use the Manelli Scale viscosity score as your guide →

Best ForClients who want the most natural-looking, barely-there highlights. Excellent as a standalone for fine-haired clients, or combined with other techniques to add dimension.

Stitch 5: Microlights

Stitch 05

Microlights

What it is: A middle ground between babylights and a traditional weave. Sections of two to five strands, leaving small amounts of hair between each section.

What it produces: Fine, diffused highlights with more coverage than babylights but more subtlety than a standard weave. Dimensional and natural-looking without the extreme time investment of true babylights.

What to watch: Like babylights, monitor processing time carefully to avoid over-processing where fine sections are close together.

Best ForClients who want something more noticeable than babylights but softer than a standard weave. Works beautifully as a base for blending techniques or color correction.

Stitch 6: The Combination Technique

Stitch 06

The Combination Technique

What it is: Using two or more stitch methods in the same service — most commonly combining slices for face-framing pieces with a fine weave through the body, or pairing babylights with a chunkier weave at the crown.

What it produces: The most customized, complex, and dimensional results possible from foil work. When done well, combination techniques look like no single method was used.

What to watch: Combination services require clear planning before you begin. Map out which technique goes where and why before picking up the first foil — otherwise the result reads as inconsistent rather than intentional.

Best ForHigh-end clients who want a custom result. Also useful when a client's hair has multiple needs — fine ends requiring delicate weaving, dense roots needing a stronger technique.

Stitch 7: The Zigzag

Stitch 07

The Zigzag

What it is: Instead of a straight horizontal parting at the top of the section, you part in a zigzag pattern. The hair included in the foil follows the zigzag line rather than a clean edge.

What it produces: A softer root line than a straight parting creates. The zigzag interrupts the clean edge, creating a slightly diffused transition from natural hair into the lightened section.

What to watch: A standard zigzag softens the root but is still relatively predictable — the parting follows a pattern the eye can eventually trace. For maximum diffusion, you need to go further. That's exactly what the Manelli Method does.

Best ForClients who want a softer root than a standard highlight but don't need full teasy-light diffusion. Useful for clients with visible previous highlight lines who need a less harsh re-growth look.

Stitch 8: The Manelli Method ★

Stitch 08 — Signature

The Manelli Method

What it is: My signature technique. The Manelli Method uses a double zigzag parting — one at the top of the section and one at the bottom — combined with a Dream Weaver comb to separate and weave the mid-lengths and ends before the foil is placed.

What it produces:

  • Soft, broken root line that looks like it grew in that way
  • Dimension through mid-lengths from the weaving step
  • A lived-in finish from day one
  • A grow-out that stays beautiful for four to six months
  • No shadow root required
  • No teasing or backcombing

How it's different from the standard zigzag: The single zigzag softens only the top edge. The Manelli Method zigzags the bottom edge too — which creates the diffused base. The double geometry means there is no clean starting line anywhere in the section. Add the Dream Weaver step through the body of the hair, and the result is a foil highlight that looks nothing like a foil highlight.

The Manelli Method is taught in full inside Hair B&B University, including step-by-step instruction and live application demonstrations.

Best ForColor correction, brunette-to-blonde transformations, lived-in and low-maintenance clients, fine or fragile hair. Any client who wants results that look grown-in rather than applied.

How to Choose the Right Technique for Each Client

Now that you know all eight, here's the decision framework I use at every consultation:

Client GoalRecommended Technique(s)
Bold, graphic, high-contrast brightnessSlice, Chunky Weave
Classic, consistent full coverageTraditional Weave
Most natural, barely-there highlightsBabylights, Microlights
Lived-in, low-maintenance, long grow-outManelli Method
Fully custom, multi-zone resultCombination
Color correction — banding or spottinessManelli Method
Dark hair, brunette-to-blondeManelli Method, Traditional Weave
Fine or fragile hairBabylights, Microlights, Manelli Method
The Deeper Framework
Ask yourself four questions at every consultation: (1) What is the client's goal? (2) What is their starting level and color history? (3) What are their maintenance habits and budget? (4) What does their hair texture and density allow? Run the client through all four and the right technique usually becomes obvious.

Once you've chosen your technique, make sure your lightener is matched to the service. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most natural-looking foil highlight technique?
For fine, delicate results, babylights and microlights produce the most natural look. For lived-in, root-diffused results on darker hair, the Manelli Method produces the most natural result.
What is the Manelli Method?
The Manelli Method is a foil highlight technique created by Mirella Manelli that uses a double zigzag parting — at both the top and bottom of each section — plus a Dream Weaver comb through the mids and ends, to create soft, lived-in roots without teasing or backcombing. Read the full guide →
What is the difference between babylights and microlights?
Babylights are the finest possible weave — one to three strands into the foil. Microlights are slightly thicker, two to five strands, creating more coverage than babylights while still producing a fine, soft result.
What technique should I use for color correction?
The Manelli Method is the primary technique for banding and spotty color correction. The double zigzag interrupts the geometric pattern of the banding, making it visually disappear into the new result.
Can I combine techniques in the same service?
Yes — the Combination Technique covers exactly this. Pairing slices at the face frame with fine weaves through the interior produces the most customized, complex results. Plan the zones before you begin.
How many foil techniques should a working stylist know?
I recommend being confident in at least four to five: a slice, a traditional weave, babylights, a zigzag variant, and the Manelli Method. That range covers the vast majority of client requests you'll encounter.
What bleach works best for fine foil techniques like babylights?
You need a lightener with excellent viscosity — one that stays exactly where you put it without migrating onto sections you didn't intend to touch. Use the Manelli Scale viscosity score to compare products before you buy.
