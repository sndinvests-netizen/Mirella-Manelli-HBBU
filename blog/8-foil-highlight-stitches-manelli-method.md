## Introduction

Here's a question I ask every student who comes through Hair B&B University: how many foil techniques do you actually use on a regular basis?

Most stylists answer honestly. Two, maybe three. A slice when they want something clean. A weave for fullness. A zigzag when they want a softer root. And they've been cycling through the same rotation since cosmetology school, because nobody ever sat them down and showed them the full picture.

There are eight distinct foil highlight stitch methods that belong in every professional colorist's vocabulary. Each one produces a different result — different root diffusion, different coverage, different texture and movement in the finished color. Understanding all eight doesn't mean you'll use all eight every week. It means you'll know which one to reach for when a specific client sits down in your chair with a specific goal.

In this post, I'm breaking down all eight, starting with the fundamentals most of you already know and building toward the technique I developed myself — the **Manelli Method** — which changes the way most stylists think about lived-in color after the first time they use it.

Bookmark this one. It's the reference post I wish someone had given me early in my career.

## Why Your Foil Technique Matters More Than Your Product {#why-technique-matters}

I hear stylists debate bleach brands constantly. Developer ratios, processing times, integrity scores — and all of that matters. But the truth is, two colorists can use the exact same lightener on the exact same starting level and produce completely different results based solely on how they take their sections and how they place their foils.

The product gets you to the level. The technique determines what that level looks like on the hair.

A beautifully lifted section placed with a slice will look completely different from the same lifted section placed with a babylight weave — different texture, different root, different movement, different coverage. The chemistry was identical. The foil placement made the difference.

This is why technique is the foundation that everything else builds on. It's also why stylists who invest in expanding their foiling vocabulary consistently outperform those who stay in a two-method comfort zone — not just in results, but in the range of clients they can confidently serve and the price points they can command.

## Stitch 1: The Slice {#slice}

**What it is:** A slice takes every strand of hair within the section into the foil. There is no weaving — the entire thickness of hair between two partings is isolated and lightened.

**What it produces:** Maximum coverage and high contrast. Slices are bright, bold, and graphic. They create clean, chunky pieces of color with visible separation from the natural base.

**Best for:** Clients who want high-impact brightness, dramatic contrast, or a more editorial look. Also useful in a corrective context when you need to fully lift a specific section.

**What to watch:** Because slices lift every strand in the section, they can look dense and heavy if overused. Best used in targeted placement rather than as the only technique throughout a full head.

## Stitch 2: The Chunky Weave {#chunky-weave}

**What it is:** A weave where the comb picks up larger, thicker sections of hair to go into the foil, leaving thicker sections out. The weave alternates between "in" and "out" but at a coarser scale than a traditional weave.

**What it produces:** Bold, visible highlights with clear separation between lighter and darker sections. Less graphic than a slice because some hair is left out, but still a high-contrast result.

**Best for:** Clients who want dimension but with visible, chunky brightness rather than a fine, diffused look. Great for clients who want a retro or fashion-forward aesthetic, or clients with very thick hair who need significant lift distributed throughout the section.

**What to watch:** Chunky weaves can look dated if not balanced with face-framing placement or a strong tone. Work thoughtfully on fine or thin hair — large patches of lifted color can create the appearance of reduced density.

---

## Stitch 3: The Traditional Weave {#traditional-weave}

**What it is:** The standard foil weave most stylists learn in cosmetology school. The comb alternates picking up fine-to-medium sections of hair — into the foil, left out, into the foil — creating an even, regular pattern throughout the section.

**What it produces:** The classic highlight look. Even distribution of lighter and darker strands, consistent coverage, predictable result. This is what most clients picture when they ask for a "full head of highlights."

**Best for:** Clients who want consistent coverage and a reliable, uniform result. Works well on most hair types and is the most adaptable technique to different foil densities (more foils = more coverage, fewer foils = less).

**What to watch:** The traditional weave produces a regular, predictable pattern that can read as "done" rather than "grown-in" — especially at the root line. For clients who want a more natural result, this technique benefits from combining with a softer root approach.

## Stitch 4: Babylights {#babylights}

**What it is:** An ultra-fine weave that takes very small, delicate sections of hair into the foil, leaving the majority of hair out. The sections are typically one to three strands wide.

**What it produces:** The finest, most natural highlight possible. Babylights create the effect of natural childhood hair — where sunlight has kissed individual strands rather than chunks of hair. The result is incredibly dimensional, especially on clients with thinner or finer hair.

**Best for:** Clients who want the most natural-looking, barely-there highlights. Also excellent as a standalone service for fine-haired clients who would look overdone with any heavier technique, or combined with other techniques to add dimension without obvious highlights.

**What to watch:** Babylights are time-intensive — the sections are small, so you're placing significantly more foils. They are not a budget service and should be priced accordingly. On very dark or coarse hair, the subtlety of babylights may not deliver the lift clients expect.

---

## Stitch 5: Microlights {#microlights}

**What it is:** A middle ground between babylights and a traditional weave. Finer than traditional but not as delicate as babylights — sections of two to five strands, leaving small amounts of hair out between each section.

**What it produces:** Fine, diffused highlights with more coverage than babylights but more subtlety than a traditional weave. Microlights are dimensional and natural-looking without the extreme time investment of babylights.

**Best for:** Clients who want something more noticeable than babylights but softer than a standard weave. Works beautifully as a base for blending techniques or color correction.

**What to watch:** Like babylights, microlights require careful placement to avoid over-processing in areas where fine sections are close together. Monitor processing time carefully.

---

## Stitch 6: The Combination Technique {#combination}

**What it is:** Using two or more stitch methods in the same service — most commonly combining slices for face-framing pieces with a fine weave through the body of the hair, or pairing babylights with a chunkier weave at the crown.

**What it produces:** The most customized, complex, and dimensional results possible from foil work. When done well, combination techniques look like no single method was used — they look like the hair is just deeply multi-dimensional.

**Best for:** High-end clients who want a custom result and are willing to pay for the additional chair time and creativity. Also useful when a client's hair has multiple needs — fine ends that need delicate weaving, dense roots that need a stronger technique.

**What to watch:** Combination services require clear planning before you begin. Map out which technique goes where and why before picking up the first foil, otherwise the result can read as inconsistent rather than intentional.

---

## Stitch 7: The Zigzag {#zigzag}

**What it is:** Instead of a straight horizontal parting at the top of the section, you part in a zigzag pattern. The hair included in the foil follows the zigzag line rather than a clean edge.

**What it produces:** A softer root line than a straight or woven parting creates. The zigzag interrupts the clean edge where the highlight begins, creating a slightly diffused, less abrupt transition from natural hair into the lightened section.

**Best for:** Clients who want a softer root than a standard highlight but don't need the full diffusion of a teasy light effect. Also useful for clients with visible previous highlight lines who need a technique that doesn't make the re-growth look harsh.

**What to watch:** A standard zigzag softens the root but is still relatively predictable — the parting, while irregular, follows a pattern that the eye can eventually trace. For maximum diffusion, you need to go further.

---

## Stitch 8: The Manelli Method {#manelli-method}

**What it is:** My signature technique. The Manelli Method uses a **double zigzag parting** — one at the top of the section and one at the bottom — combined with a **Dream Weaver comb** to separate and weave the mid-lengths and ends before the foil is placed.

This is the technique that ends the chapter on the single zigzag. The double zigzag creates a parting geometry that no other technique produces — and the result at the root is genuinely different from anything you've likely seen come out of a foil before.

**What it produces:** A soft, broken root line that looks like it grew in that way. Not diffused in the way a shadow root is applied on top — diffused because the parting itself is irregular at both the top and bottom of the section. When the lightener processes and the foil comes out, the root zone reads as multi-tonal, dimensional, and completely natural.

The Dream Weaver step through the mid-lengths adds a second layer of complexity — variation through the body of the hair that creates depth and makes the entire result look like light playing through the hair rather than like product was applied.

**The result:**
- Soft root diffusion with no hard edges
- Dimension through mid-lengths from the weaving step
- A lived-in finish from day one
- A grow-out that stays beautiful for four to six months
- No shadow root required
- No teasing or backcombing

**Best for:**
- Color correction clients with banding or patchy color
- Brunette-to-blonde transformations where a natural root is essential
- Lived-in and low-maintenance clients who can't come in frequently
- Clients with fine or fragile hair who can't tolerate mechanical teasing
- Any client who wants a result that looks grown-in rather than applied

**How it's different from the standard zigzag:** The single zigzag softens the top edge of the section. The Manelli Method zigzags the bottom edge too — which is what creates the diffused base. The double geometry means there is no clean starting line for the color anywhere in the section. Add the Dream Weaver weaving through the body of the hair, and the result is a foil highlight that looks nothing like a foil highlight.

## How to Choose the Right Technique for Each Client {#choosing}

Now that you know all eight, the question is how to decide. Here's the framework I use at every consultation:

**Step 1: What is the client's goal?**
Bold and graphic → slice or chunky weave.
Natural and dimensional → traditional weave, microlights, or babylights.
Lived-in and low-maintenance → Manelli Method.
Maximum subtlety → babylights or microlights.
Custom and complex → combination.

**Step 2: What is the client's starting level and history?**
Dark virgin hair → techniques that allow controlled foil application (Manelli Method, traditional weave, combination).
Previously colored with inconsistency → Manelli Method or combination with correction approach.
Lighter or previously highlighted → more flexibility across all eight techniques.

**Step 3: What are the client's maintenance habits and budget?**
Low maintenance, low frequency → techniques with longer grow-out windows (Manelli Method, babylights).
High maintenance, regular appointments → traditional weave, combination.

**Step 4: What does the client's hair texture and density allow?**
Fine hair → babylights, microlights, or Manelli Method with care around saturation.
Thick/coarse hair → slice, chunky weave, Manelli Method with strong developer.
Fragile or compromised → avoid anything that requires mechanical disruption (backcombing, heavy weaves) — Manelli Method is the safest foil option for compromised hair.

Print this framework and put it in your consultation binder. When you run a client through all four questions, the right technique usually becomes obvious.

## Frequently Asked Questions {#faq}

**What is the most natural-looking foil highlight technique?**
For fine, delicate results, babylights and microlights produce the most natural look. For lived-in, root-diffused results, the Manelli Method produces the most natural result — especially on darker hair.

**What is the Manelli Method?**
The Manelli Method is a foil highlight technique created by Mirella Manelli that uses a double zigzag parting (at both the top and bottom of each section) and a Dream Weaver comb to create diffused, lived-in highlights without any teasing or backcombing.

**What is the difference between babylights and microlights?**
Babylights are the finest possible weave — one to three strands into the foil. Microlights are slightly thicker, two to five strands, creating more coverage than babylights while still producing a fine, soft result.

**What technique should I use for color correction?**
The Manelli Method is my primary technique for color correction, particularly for banding and spotty color. The double zigzag interrupts the geometric pattern of the banding, making it visually disappear into the new color result.

**How many foil techniques should a working stylist know?**
I recommend being confident in at least four to five: a slice, a traditional weave, babylights, a zigzag variant, and the Manelli Method. That range covers the vast majority of client requests you'll encounter.

**Can I combine techniques in the same service?**
Yes — this is called a combination technique and it's one of the eight methods covered above. Combining techniques (such as slices at the face frame and fine weaves through the interior) produces the most customized, complex results.
